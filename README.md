# O-1A: building a profile for a successful petition

## Executive summary

**What this is.** A working guide and evidence folder for building an O-1A (extraordinary ability, sciences, education, business and athletics) petition. Part 1 below is what to do to build the profile. The rest is the research behind it: the eight criteria, how USCIS actually reviews a petition, the 2026 environment, the required pieces that are not criteria, how to build each kind of evidence, and how letters work. `papers/` holds one folder per paper that exists, has been started, or could be written from material already on hand, because published work is one of the eight kinds of evidence.

**The three things to know.**

1. **You do not need all eight criteria; you need at least three, and three is not enough.** Evidence for three of the eight criteria (or one major internationally recognized award) only gets a petition past step 1. At step 2 the officer weighs the whole record against the real standard: sustained national or international acclaim, among the small percentage at the very top of the field. A petition can check three boxes and still be denied.
2. **Since August 2026 a thin filing can be denied outright.** Policy Alert PA-2026-05 lets officers deny without first issuing a request for evidence. File the strongest complete case the first time.
3. **Build backward from the step-2 argument.** Define who "the top of the field" is, show where the person sits, then map evidence onto criteria. Do not collect three boxes and hope.

**For a recent graduate, where to put effort.** Three criteria can be actively applied for or volunteered into: **judging, authorship and awards**. **Press** cannot be applied for but can be caused. **Critical role** and **salary** follow from job choice. **Original contributions** is earned, though adoption can be made visible. **Membership** is mostly out of reach in the first five years. Test every opportunity: *would this still mean something if nobody involved needed a visa?* Apply for anything selective and merit-based, because the selection process does the proving; skip anything that accepts everyone or charges to enter, because selectivity is the whole evidentiary value.

**Not legal advice.** These are research notes dated 2026-10-28. The attorneys on a case are the authority. Use this to have a sharper conversation with them. I (the AI that assembled this folder) have not independently verified the 2026 policy details or the approval statistics in Part 6, the award deadlines, or the practitioner claims in Part 7; they come from the research notes and the sources listed at the end, and should be checked against the USCIS pages before anyone relies on them.

## Part 1: What to do to build the profile

Start now; most of this takes months.

1. **Start an evidence log today,** organised by criterion, capturing documents when they are created and not reconstructing them at filing. Records made at the time of an event carry more weight under the 2026 guidance.
2. **Pick the comparison set.** Write down who "the top of the field" is and where the person sits in it, with evidence. Everything else hangs from this.
3. **Aim for four or five credible criteria,** not three borderline ones.
4. **Judging (criterion 4)** is usually the fastest to build. Get on reviewer pools for journals and conferences, abstract-review panels, dissertation committees and grant panels. Keep the invitation and the proof of completion for every review. Repeat invitations from the same venue show sustained recognition.
5. **Authorship (criterion 6)** is easy at step 1, but only strong venues help at step 2. For each paper, document the venue: acceptance rate, impact factor or field ranking. A paper in a pay-to-publish venue meets the plain wording and adds little.
6. **Original contributions (criterion 5)** is the hardest. Published or funded shows originality, not significance. What moves it: independent citations that build on the work, adoption by others (for software, downloads, forks and dependent projects), commercialisation or licensing, and expert letters that say what changed in the field because of the work, each claim tied to an exhibit.
7. **Critical role (criterion 7)** needs two proofs: the role was critical or essential, and the organisation (or the specific division) has a distinguished reputation. A detailed letter from the director or principal investigator explaining why the role mattered is what the Policy Manual describes.
8. **Press (criterion 3)** must be about the person and their work, with title, date and author, and the outlet's audience documented. University news stories are self-generated and weak; paid placements are a red flag. Earned coverage takes months.
9. **Awards (criterion 1)** need the selection criteria, applicant pool, number of winners and who judged documented. **Membership (criterion 2)** counts only for selective, expert-judged grades such as Fellow; do not spend exhibits on dues-based membership. **Salary (criterion 8)** needs a comparison against the specific occupation in the specific metro using government wage data.
10. **Avoid double-counting.** If one project generates the press article, the talk and the critical-role letter, the officer sees one achievement three times. Spread criteria across distinct accomplishments over time.
11. **Do not manufacture.** Papers produced to hit a count, in weak venues, help at step 1 and can erode credibility at step 2.
12. **Find independent recommenders.** Authors who cite and build on the work are ideal, and cold outreach to them is normal practice. Letters from supervisors, collaborators and co-authors carry less weight.
13. **Plan for the green card.** O-1A evidence overlaps heavily with EB-1A. Some practitioners advise against spending every top independent recommender on the O-1.
14. **Have a contingency** for an outright denial, since 2026 procedure no longer guarantees a request for evidence first.

### Where a recent graduate should put effort

| Criterion | Can you go get it? | Time to build | Best moves | Main trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Judging | Yes | Fastest: months | Conference and journal reviewing; professional hackathons and pitch competitions | Student events; invitations without proof of completion |
| Authorship | Yes | Months (peer-review cycles) | Peer-reviewed papers and proceedings; technical articles in professional magazines | Preprints, op-eds, corporate blogs, pay-to-publish venues |
| Awards | Yes, by applying | Annual cycles | Free, selective, expert-judged honors; best-paper awards; national competitions | Pay-to-enter, public-vote, and many-category awards |
| Press | Indirectly | Months to years | Newsworthy work, pitched to trade and professional reporters | Paid placements, self-authored pieces, institutional press releases alone |
| Original contributions | Earned | Years | Make adoption visible: open source, datasets, independent citations | Counting outputs instead of showing uptake |
| Critical role | Through job choice | Tied to employment | Roles at organizations whose reputation can be documented | Titles without documented impact; obscure organizations |
| High salary | Through job choice | Tied to employment | Top-paying employers; equity for founders | Comparing against the wrong occupation or metro |
| Membership | Rarely, early career | Years | Mostly skip for now | Dues-based or experience-based memberships |

**Common ideas that need adjusting**

- **Hackathon judging** counts when it is judging professionals. Judging student hackathons is consistently treated as weak.
- **Writing for a general-audience magazine** does not hit the criterion it seems to. The press criterion requires material about the person, so self-authored pieces do not count there, and general-audience opinion pieces are usually not "scholarly" enough for the authorship criterion.
- **Books** are slow and usually land in the wrong criterion. A technical book from an established publisher, especially one adopted for courses, can help. A trade or self-published book mostly does not.
- **Papers in progress** count only once they are published after peer review, before the filing date. Preprints and papers under review do not.

## Part 2: The short answer, and the correction

The "8 things" are almost certainly the eight evidentiary criteria in the O-1A regulation, 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(B). But "8 things you need to do" is wrong in two directions.

**You don't need all eight.** A petition needs evidence satisfying at least three of them, or a single major internationally recognized award (Nobel-level) in place of all of them.

**Three isn't enough.** USCIS states plainly that the criteria are not the standard for the classification; they are the mechanism for testing whether the standard is met. After counting criteria, the officer weighs the entire record to decide whether the person has sustained national or international acclaim and is among the small percentage at the very top of the field.

If the field were the arts or film/TV, the category would be O-1B, which has six different criteria. Everything below assumes O-1A, which fits a research and technical profile.

## Part 3: The eight criteria

| # | Criterion (paraphrasing the regulation) | What tends to count | What USCIS discounts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field | Awards from well-known national institutions or professional associations; some dissertation awards; awards for presentations at recognized conferences. Team awards count if the person is a named recipient. | Awards limited to one locality, employer, or school. USCIS weighs the selection criteria, significance, number of awardees, and who was eligible to compete. |
| 2 | Membership in associations requiring outstanding achievement, as judged by recognized experts | Fellow-level grades where experts judge nominees. The Policy Manual's own examples are IEEE Fellow and AAAI Fellow. | Membership based on a degree, years of experience, paying dues, subscribing to publications, or job requirements. Ordinary IEEE or ACM membership does nothing. |
| 3 | Published material about the person and their work, in professional or major trade publications or major media | Articles that substantially discuss the person's work. Must include title, date, and author. | Passing mentions or brief citations. The outlet must qualify as professional, major trade, or major media; audience and circulation matter. |
| 4 | Judging the work of others, individually or on a panel | Peer review for journals and conferences; conference abstract review; doctoral dissertation committees; government grant review panels | An invitation without proof the review was actually completed. |
| 5 | Original scientific, scholarly, or business-related contributions of major significance | Citation levels indicative of significance; widespread commentary from others in the field; patents with uptake; commercialization; contributions to software, data, or protocol repositories with documented impact; detailed expert letters corroborated by the record; letters from interested government agencies | Work that was merely funded, patented, or published. That shows originality, not significance. |
| 6 | Authorship of scholarly articles in professional journals or other major media | Being a listed author (not necessarily first or sole); peer-reviewed published conference proceedings | Nothing at the criterion stage: listed authorship alone satisfies it. Venue quality gets judged later, in the totality review. |
| 7 | Employment in a critical or essential capacity for organizations with a distinguished reputation | Research roles in distinguished departments or institutions; named investigator on merit-based grants (including SBIR); founder of a well-funded startup; a key supporting role explained in detail by the director or principal investigator | Titles without substance. USCIS evaluates duties and performance, not the job title. The organization must separately be shown to be distinguished. |
| 8 | High salary or remuneration, past or prospective | Pay records, tax returns, offer letters, compared against occupation- and location-specific wage data (BLS, CareerOneStop) | Broad wage categories spanning unrelated jobs, unreliable self-reported salary sites, foreign salaries simply converted to dollars. |

**Comparable evidence.** If a criterion doesn't readily apply to the person's occupation, the petitioner can substitute evidence of comparable significance, but must explain both why the criterion doesn't fit and why the substitute is comparable. The Policy Manual's examples: presenting at a major trade show in place of scholarly articles for someone in industry, and highly valued startup equity in place of salary for a founder. Comparable evidence doesn't lower the count; it's still three separate criteria.

## Part 4: The two-step review (where petitions actually die)

**Step 1 is counting.** The officer checks whether the evidence falls within the plain language of at least three criteria. No judgment yet about whether it shows extraordinary ability.

**Step 2 is the totality determination.** The officer weighs everything, including evidence that fits no criterion, against the actual standard: sustained acclaim, top of the field. Practitioners trace the two-step structure to *Kazarian v. USCIS*, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010), an EB-1A case; the Policy Manual roots it in the preamble to the 1994 O rule. The standard is preponderance of the evidence, judged on quality rather than volume (*Matter of Chawathe*, 25 I&N Dec. 369 (AAO 2010)).

The Policy Manual names factors that help at step 2 even though they aren't criteria:

1. Publication in highly ranked journals (by impact factor, for example), especially as senior author, sole author, or most significant contributor.
2. A total citation rate or h-index that is high relative to the field, with the explicit caveat that this matters less early in a career.
3. Employment or research at leading institutions, such as Carnegie R1/R2 universities, comparable foreign institutions, or highly ranked universities.
4. Unsolicited invitations to speak at nationally or internationally recognized conferences.
5. Being named as an investigator on a peer-reviewed, competitively funded U.S. government grant.
6. A detailed letter from an interested government agency attesting to the person's acclaim.

"Sustained" carries no age floor. A beneficiary can be early-career and still qualify, but the acclaim has to be maintained, not a single peak.

**Strategic implication:** build the case from the step-2 argument backward. Define the comparison set ("top of the field" relative to whom?), show where the person sits in it, then map evidence onto criteria. Petitions built the other way (collect three boxes, then hope) are the ones that draw requests for evidence.

## Part 5: Required pieces that aren't criteria

If a lawyer is listing the filing package rather than the criteria, this is where "8 things" might come from. Worth asking.

1. **A U.S. petitioner.** A U.S. employer, a U.S. agent, or a foreign employer acting through a U.S. agent files Form I-129. The beneficiary can't be their own petitioner.
2. **A written advisory opinion (consultation)** from a peer group, including labor organizations, or a person with expertise in the field. It can be waived only if no appropriate peer group exists. It isn't binding on USCIS.
3. **The contract**, or a written summary of the terms of an oral agreement.
4. **An explanation of the events or activities**, with start and end dates and any itinerary, covering the validity period requested.
5. **Continuing work in the area of extraordinary ability.** USCIS reads "field" broadly: related occupations with shared skills, knowledge, or expertise count. This matters for anyone moving from academic research into industry or into founding a company.

Timing: the petition can be filed no more than a year before the start date, and USCIS asks for at least 45 days of lead time. Initial approval runs up to three years, with extensions in increments of up to one year. Premium processing is available.

## Part 6: The 2026 environment

**Approval rates are falling.** O-category approvals were 91.0% through Q3 of FY2026, the lowest since FY2021, after four years in the 93-95% range. Denials in FY2026's first three quarters already exceed the full FY2025 total. Requests for evidence (RFEs) are running around 23-24% of adjudications, up from about 19% a year earlier. USCIS doesn't publish O-1A and O-1B separately.

**Policy Alert PA-2026-05 (August 5, 2026) changed the procedural rules:**

1. Officers now have full discretion to deny without first issuing an RFE or Notice of Intent to Deny when required initial evidence is missing or the record fails to establish eligibility. It applies to anything pending or filed on or after August 5.
2. RFE response windows can be set shorter than 12 weeks, and the extra 14-day mailing allowance for notices sent abroad is gone.
3. A partial RFE response is treated as a request to decide on the existing record.
4. Affidavits are now described as generally tertiary evidence. Their weight depends on whether they state the affiant's relationship to the parties, the basis of the affiant's knowledge, and specific facts rather than conclusions. Officers are told to compare them against the rest of the record for consistency, and the guidance notes that records created at the time of an event, in the regular course of business, can carry more weight.

That fourth point bears directly on support letters. The guidance targets affidavits offered in place of primary or secondary evidence, and it isn't yet clear how strictly officers will apply it to expert testimonial letters. But the O regulations already require something similar: expert affidavits must describe the beneficiary's recognition and ability in factual terms and explain the affiant's expertise and how they came to know the facts (8 CFR 214.2(o)(2)(iii)(B)). Both rules point the same way, so write to that standard regardless.

**The political context.** The September 2025 proclamation imposing a $100,000 payment on certain new H-1B petitions, along with wage-weighted H-1B selection for FY2027, has pushed demand toward the O-1A. That shift has drawn criticism: some commentators allege an industry has formed to market the O-1A as an H-1B workaround for ordinary profiles. Defenders respond that the O-1A bar remains far higher than the H-1B's and that the category is uncapped by design. The practical consequence is the same either way: officers are seeing more marginal petitions, and a strong one has to distinguish itself clearly.

**Implication:** "file thin and fix it in the RFE" is no longer a strategy. File the strongest, complete case the first time, and plan the contingency if it's denied outright.

## Part 7: Building the portfolio, criterion by criterion

Ordered roughly from most to least accessible for a researcher.

**Judging.** Usually the fastest criterion to build, because opportunities recur and accumulate. Two conditions do all the work: the person judged the work of professionals in their own or an allied field, and there is proof the judging actually happened. What to pursue:

- **Peer review for conferences and journals.** The strongest form. Many major conferences recruit reviewers through volunteer forms or nominations from area chairs, and workshops are an easier entry point. For journals, an editor or an established reviewer can recommend someone; that is where a mentor can help directly.
- **Professional hackathons, pitch competitions and demo days,** where entrants are working professionals or startups, the event uses a formal rubric, and judges are chosen for expertise.
- **Grant or program review panels,** when available. Rare early in a career, but strong.

Avoid or discount student hackathons, class project showcases, and anything closer to mentoring than scoring. Practitioners draw a firm line between judging (assessing work against criteria and influencing outcomes) and mentoring or hosting (giving advice, holding office hours). Be skeptical of services that broker judging slots, or events that seem to exist partly to supply judges; officers check whether the competition itself is credible. Keep for each: the invitation; proof of completion (scorecards, rubrics, review-submitted confirmations, an organizer letter); and something showing the event's scale and selectivity. Practitioners report officers increasingly want completed evaluations, not just invitations. The Policy Manual's examples are peer review, conference abstract review, dissertation committees and government grant panels. Volume helps at step 1; venue prestige helps at step 2.

**Authorship.** Easy at step 1, but it only matters at step 2 if the venues are strong. A paper in a pay-to-publish journal satisfies the plain language of the criterion, yet the Policy Manual says it won't necessarily support a finding of acclaim. (Paying open-access fees at a selective journal doesn't count against you.) For each paper, document the venue: acceptance rate, impact factor, or field ranking.

- **Papers in progress** are the core, but timing is unforgiving. USCIS expects eligibility to be established at the time of filing, and practitioners report officers treating arXiv preprints as not yet scholarly articles because peer review is not complete. A paper under review on filing day does not exist for the petition. Published proceedings from recognized conferences do count; the Policy Manual says so explicitly.
- **Articles in general-audience magazines** have two problems. The press criterion requires material about the person, so a piece the person writes does not qualify. For authorship the article must be scholarly (written for learned people in the field) and appear in a professional publication, major trade publication or major media; general-interest op-eds are usually argued under other criteria. A better target is a professional or trade magazine aimed at practitioners (in computing, something like IEEE Spectrum or Communications of the ACM; examples from the research notes, not USCIS's), with a technical piece rather than an opinion piece. General-audience writing can still support the overall picture at step 2 and may lead to coverage about the person.
- **Books** are slow and often mapped to the wrong criterion. Major textbook authorship supports the criterion when the book is adopted at multiple institutions and reviewed in the scholarly literature. A technical book from an established publisher can help, and reviews of it in professional or major outlets would be published material about the person. A self-published or general trade book adds little. With a one-to-two-year production cycle, a book is rarely the efficient move for a near-term O-1; think of it as an EB-1A-horizon project.

**Critical role.** Two halves, and both have to be proved: the role was critical or essential, and the organization (or the specific division) has a distinguished reputation. For academic settings, national rankings, research classification, and government grants establish reputation. For startups, funding commensurate with stage and industry. The Policy Manual explicitly contemplates a detailed letter from the director or PI explaining why a supporting role mattered relative to others in similar positions, and the person need not have been directly employed by the organization. For a recent graduate this is a job-choice decision: favor organizations whose distinction is easy to prove (rankings, funding, media coverage) and a supervisor who can describe specific impact in a letter.

**Original contributions of major significance.** The hardest criterion and the one most often lost on appeal. Being published or funded shows originality, not significance. It cannot be applied for, but much of what makes it provable is documentation habit:

- Release code, models or datasets publicly and track downloads, forks, dependent projects and who uses them. The Policy Manual explicitly lists contributions to repositories with documented impact.
- Track independent citations, especially papers that build on the work rather than merely list it.
- Save evidence of adoption as it happens: emails from teams using the tool, industry mentions, integrations into other products.
- Commercialization or licensing, and expert letters that say specifically what changed in the field because of the work, each claim backed by an exhibit.

**Press.** It has to be about the person and their work, not a topic story with a passing mention. Title, date, and author are required, and the outlet's audience or circulation should be documented. It cannot be applied for, but it can be caused: do work that is newsworthy, make it legible (a clear write-up, a demo, a public release), and pitch it to reporters covering that beat at trade and professional outlets. A university news office story is self-published by the institution and carries little weight on its own, though reporters do pick those stories up. Earned coverage is a months-long relationship with journalists, not a weeks-long campaign. Paid placements and press releases do not count.

**Awards.** The question USCIS and the AAO ask is whether the award recognizes excellence nationally or internationally, and the petition has to prove it: rules, judging criteria, number of nominees and recipients, judges' qualifications, past winners, geographic scope, and press coverage. An award open across an entire research university or a national professional body can qualify where a department-only award won't. The AAO has rejected placements at a large international competition when the selection process was not documented, rejected public-vote magazine contests, and rejected venture funding and accelerator admission as awards, reasoning that an investment decision is not a prize for excellence.

- **Free, selective, expert-judged honors that accept self-nominations.** *MIT Technology Review Innovators Under 35:* free, self-nominations encouraged, nominees under 35; the 2026 list drew 550 nominations for 35 spots, chosen by editors with input from an expert panel; nominations for the 2027 list open by early December 2026; regional editions exist (Europe, Asia Pacific, Latin America, Middle East and North Africa). *Forbes 30 Under 30:* free, self-nominations considered equally, nominees 29 or younger; the 2027 U.S. cycle closed September 1, 2026; regional lists run on their own calendars; Forbes weighs scale, impact and innovation, so it suits founders better than researchers. The odds are long (roughly 6% of Innovators Under 35 nominees made the 2026 list), but the cost is an hour. Both produce a published profile; the attorneys decide whether to claim it under awards, press or both, remembering that one achievement presented twice reads as one achievement.
- **Recognitions earned through the work.** Best paper, best demo or best poster at nationally or internationally recognized conferences (the Policy Manual names awards for conference presentations). Wins or top placements in national or international technical competitions with formal judging: professional hackathons, pitch competitions, data science competitions with ranked leaderboards; document the number of entrants and how judging worked.
- **Traps.** Pay-to-enter business awards with entry fees, hundreds of categories and many winners (the Stevie and Globee Awards are frequently cited) are weak. Public-vote and popularity awards have been rejected. Funding, grants and accelerator admission presented as awards are weak as awards; competitive government grants are more useful as step-2 evidence and for the critical-role criterion.

**Membership.** Only selective, expert-judged grades count, and the fellow-level grades that do count typically arrive mid-career, so for a recent graduate put effort here last. Don't spend exhibits on dues-based, degree-based or experience-based memberships. Board or committee service at an organisation does not satisfy this criterion, which requires an outstanding-achievement gate judged by experts. Some practitioners argue selective accelerator cohorts as memberships when acceptance rates are documented, but that is contested, and the AAO has rejected accelerator admission as an award.

**Salary.** Compare against the specific occupation in the specific metro using government wage data. Vague comparisons fail. Early-career pay rarely clears the bar outside top-paying employers; founders can argue equity as comparable evidence.

**Evidence hygiene that applies across all of them:**

1. **Avoid double-counting.** If one project generates the press article, the conference talk, and the critical-role letter, the officer sees one achievement presented three times. Spread the criteria across distinct accomplishments, so the record shows multiple contributions over time.
2. **Keep a running evidence log, starting now,** organized by criterion, capturing documentation when it's created rather than reconstructing it at filing. Given the 2026 guidance on contemporaneous records, this matters more than it used to.
3. **Plan for the green card.** O-1A evidence overlaps heavily with EB-1A, the extraordinary-ability green card. Some practitioners advise against spending every top independent recommender on the O-1 so there are fresh voices for the later petition.

## Part 8: Letters

Three different documents get called "letters":

1. **The advisory opinion (consultation).** Required. From a peer group or a person with expertise. Advisory, not binding.
2. **Expert or testimonial letters.** Evidence for specific criteria (especially original contributions and critical role) and for the totality argument.
3. **The petitioner's support letter.** Describes the U.S. role and why it's in the field of acclaim.

**Independent versus dependent.** Letters from experts who know the work by reputation and have never worked with the beneficiary carry more weight than letters from supervisors, collaborators, or co-authors. The practitioner norm is five to eight letters with a meaningful share from independent experts, though no rule sets a number.

**A recommender who is a dependent one** (a former professor, a long-time co-author, the founder of an organisation the person serves) still adds real value, because dependent letters supply granular, verifiable specifics an outside expert can't know. But such letters can't be the backbone of the case, and a stack of letters from the person's own circle reads as exactly that.

**What makes a letter carry weight** (synthesizing the O regulation on expert affidavits, the PA-2026-05 guidance, and how the AAO has treated letters):

1. State the relationship plainly, including co-authorship and any teaching relationship. Concealing it is worse than disclosing it.
2. Explain the basis of your knowledge: what you observed directly, and when.
3. Give specific facts: the problem, what the person personally did as distinct from the team, what changed as a result, who adopted or cited it, with numbers wherever possible.
4. Establish your own credentials as the basis for judging significance.
5. Avoid legal conclusions ("he meets criterion 5," "he has extraordinary ability"). Describe the facts; let the attorneys map them to criteria.
6. Make it unique. The AAO has discounted letters that share template language. If the attorneys draft letters for signature, which is common, rewrite yours in your own voice with your own specifics.
7. Keep it consistent with the record. Every claim in the letter should point to an exhibit.

**High-leverage moves for a research collaborator or former advisor:**

1. **The principal-investigator letter for the critical-role criterion,** if the person's work on the research was genuinely critical, paired with documentation of the program's reputation.
2. **Open judging doors.** Nominate the person to reviewer pools for venues where you review or sit on committees.
3. **Find independent recommenders.** Pull the papers that cite the person's work. Authors who built on it are ideal independent experts.
4. **Venue strategy for co-authored work.** Aim future papers at venues whose selectivity can be documented. A paper that only satisfies the authorship criterion adds little.
5. **Don't manufacture.** Papers produced mainly to hit a count, in weak venues, help at step 1 and can erode credibility at step 2. The officer reads the whole record.

## Part 9: Questions to put to the lawyers

1. What exactly were the "8 things": the criteria, or the filing package?
2. Which criteria are you claiming, and which is the weakest? Aim for four or five credible criteria, not three borderline ones.
3. What is the step-2 argument? What is the comparison set for "top of the field," and what evidence places the person in it?
4. Who is the petitioner, and how does the U.S. role connect to the field of acclaim?
5. Who is writing the advisory opinion?
6. How many letters, how many independent, and who is drafting them?
7. Given PA-2026-05, is the filing complete enough to survive a denial without an RFE, and what is the contingency plan if it's denied?
8. Is the evidence being assembled with a later EB-1A petition in mind?

## Part 10: A caution if this becomes a program

If this guidance is used for a cohort of recent graduates and not for one person, two risks scale with it.

- **Network signatures.** If many petitions from one mentor's circle cite the same judging events, the same small venues and letters from the same people, officers can see the pattern. Each person's record needs to look like their own, because it should be.
- **In-house opportunities.** Judging panels, awards or publications created through organizations the program's own organisers run are in-network evidence: easy to discount and, in the current climate, a credibility risk for everyone who relies on them. The strongest help is opening doors to external, selective venues, not creating venues.

None of that argues against helping people build real records. It argues for steering them toward the hardest selective opportunities they can plausibly win.

## The papers folder

`papers/` has one folder per paper, 24 at last count. Published work feeds criterion 6 (authorship) directly, and, where there is documented use by others, criterion 5 (original contributions; the Policy Manual names software, data and protocol repositories). It feeds step 2 only if the venue is strong. See `papers/README.md` for the index and each paper's status. A paper that exists only as a draft earns nothing yet, and a draft in a weak venue earns little.

Status words used in `papers/`: **Drafted** (full prose, claims and citations still to check), **Shell** (structure and abstract, empty slots where findings go), **Design only** (a plan, no study run), **Material only** (data or code, no paper text).
